# Journalist Outreach That Gets Replies

A method for founder- and operator-led earned media. Most cold pitches to journalists fail the same way: they lead with the company, they aren't anchored to anything the reporter actually wrote, and they open with "happy to be a source" and no reason to reply. This fixes all three. Every draft anchors to the reporter's recent work, leads with the news value to their readers, and closes on a concrete give-plus-ask.

## The four types

- **A. First contact (no news yet).** Get on their radar before you need coverage. Do not pitch.
- **B. News pitch.** A specific announcement worth saying: partnership, product milestone, report.
- **C. Byline pitch.** A contributed article from your exec, backed by a real existing POV or post.
- **D. Reactive pitch.** A story just broke in your space and you have a fast, specific expert take. Speed is the point - within a few hours.

## The non-negotiable rules

1. **Anchor to a specific recent article.** First contact and pitches must reference a specific piece the journalist published recently (roughly the last 3 weeks) that you can genuinely comment on. Generic beat-level intros do not qualify. Confirm the byline is actually recent at the source - a maintained contact list can go stale. Record the anchor's title, date, and URL.
2. **Lead with one on-beat angle, not your company.** Open on the news value to their readers. One line on your relevance, not three.
3. **The them:us ratio.** Count sentences about their readers / the story vs sentences about you. Target 3:1 minimum. If it's 1:1, rewrite.
4. **Give-plus-ask close.** Never open or close with "happy to be a source". Offer something concrete (a specific quote, a data point, an exclusive angle, a draft) and make one clear, low-effort ask. The give earns the ask.
5. **One reporter per outlet per story.** Never pitch competing reporters at the same outlet simultaneously. Match the angle to the publication.
6. **Run the copy through anti-slop.** Apply the `anti-slop` and `human-mannerisms` skills to every draft. A pitch should read like one person emailing another, not a machine performing an intro.

## Structures

### First contact (under 5 sentences, no ask)
- Who you are (one sentence).
- Why you're relevant to their beat, specific to their recent piece (one sentence).
- Offer to be a resource for future stories.
- No pitch.

### News pitch (under 150 words)
- What happened (one sentence).
- Why it matters to their readers, not to you.
- One line on your relevance.
- Link to the full release or supporting material.
- Offer a specific exec quote.

### Byline pitch
- A proposed, specific headline.
- 2-3 sentences on the argument: what it says, why now, why their readers care.
- One line on who would write it and their credibility.
- Note a draft is available.

### Reactive pitch (under 100 words)
- Reference the story that just broke, by name.
- Offer a specific expert comment on the angle.
- Make clear you can turn a quote around within the hour.

## The LinkedIn warm-up (before a first email)

Follow them. Engage genuinely with 1-2 recent posts (something real, not "great piece"). Wait a few days, then email. Skip this for reactive pitches where speed wins.

## The review gate (every draft)

Nothing ships as "final" until it clears one independent read for: is it anchored to a real recent piece with a URL, does it pass the them:us ratio, is the close a real give-plus-ask, and is the copy clean (anti-slop, no house-banned words, sounds like a person). One revision round, max. Strip any internal process language before it reaches the journalist.

## Track and follow up

Log every outreach: name, publication, date, type, response status. Follow up once after 5-7 business days, one sentence, no pressure. Log every response, even a decline - a "not now" is a relationship, not a rejection. Feed wins, misses, and lessons back so the list and the anchors stay current.

## What not to do

- Never send a mass pitch. Every email references something specific to that reporter.
- Never send the press release as the pitch. The release is supporting material.
- Never follow up more than once on the same pitch.
- Never pitch an outlet a story that belongs at a different one.
